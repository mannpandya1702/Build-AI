Automation advice usually fails in the same way: it shows you a dazzling 40-step
workflow that no one on your team understands, that breaks silently the first time
an input changes. The automations that actually stick are boring, small, and tied
to a number. Here are the three we build first for almost every business.

## 1. Lead routing that never drops a lead

The moment a lead comes in — a form, a call, a DM — the clock starts. Speed to
first response is one of the biggest predictors of whether you win the deal. A
routing automation:

- Captures the lead from wherever it lands
- Enriches it with what you already know
- Routes it to the right person **instantly**, in the tool they actually watch
  (Slack, your CRM, a text)

No lead sits in an inbox for three hours. That's often the highest-ROI automation
a small team can build, and it usually pays for itself in the first won deal.

## 2. Follow-ups that actually happen

Most revenue is lost in the follow-up, not the first touch. But follow-up is
exactly the kind of repetitive, easy-to-forget work humans are bad at and
automations are good at. A follow-up sequence nudges leads and customers on a
schedule — personalized from real context, not a blast — and quietly stops when
someone replies so a human can take over. **Human-in-the-loop is the point:** the
automation does the remembering; your team does the talking.

## 3. Inbox and ticket triage

A shared inbox is where small businesses drown. Triage automation reads each
incoming message, categorizes it (sales, support, billing, spam), drafts a
suggested reply grounded in your policies, and routes it — so your team opens a
sorted, half-answered queue instead of chaos. They still hit send; they just
don't start from zero.

## The rules that keep automations from betraying you

Speed isn't the goal. **Trust** is. Two rules matter more than the tooling:

1. **Human-in-the-loop where the stakes are high.** Anything touching money, legal,
   or a fragile relationship gets a human check. Automate the busywork around the
   decision, not the decision.
2. **Observable and fail-safe.** You should be able to see every run and every
   step. When something goes wrong — and it will — it should fail loudly and
   safely, never silently.

## Start with one

You don't need all three at once. Pick the one where the pain is sharpest —
usually lead routing — prove it works against a real number, and expand from
there. That's the entire method: small, measured, observable.
